# Rewriting Playbook

This file governs Mode B (standalone rewriting) and the rewrite step of Mode C (iterate). Read `references/clt-definitions.md` and `references/speech-examples.md` alongside this file before rewriting anything.

The playbook is grounded in the training protocol Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011) used to teach managers — a protocol that produced a pooled training effect of d = .62 and ~60% jumps in observers' competence ratings (Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2012, HBR).

---

## The cardinal rule: diagnosis before prescription

Do not try to insert all 9 tactics. Insert the 2–4 tactics that this specific piece is *missing* AND that are highest-leverage for its content type.

Before writing a single word of the rewrite:

1. Read the draft twice. The first read is to understand the substance and the speaker's voice. The second is to identify which tactics are already present and which are absent.
2. Identify the **category balance**. Does the draft have framing tactics but no substance? Substance but no framing? Both thin? This determines what to add. A well-formed charismatic piece has at least 2 framing tactics AND at least 2 substance tactics.
3. If you are in Mode C, read the diagnostic from the scoring pass. Use its `highest_leverage_additions` list as your shortlist. Treat its `framing_balance` and `substance_balance` notes as authoritative.
4. If you are in Mode B (no prior scoring pass), construct your own short diagnostic mentally before writing. Same logic.
5. Pick at most 4 tactics to add for content over 200 words. For content under 200 words (email range), pick at most 2. For content under 100 words, pick at most 1.

---

## What the rewrite must NOT change

This discipline comes directly from Antonakis et al. (2022). To isolate the effect of *charisma* (delivery and style) from the effect of *content* (the message itself), the field experiment used charismatic and non-charismatic versions of the same speech with the same word count, the same topics, the same information, the same structure, and the same themes. Only the style varied. The rewriter holds to the same constraint.

1. **The substance.** Every factual claim, number, commitment, decision, deadline, and piece of news in the original must remain in the rewrite, unchanged. If the original says "we are reducing the team by 15%," the rewrite says "we are reducing the team by 15%" — not "we are evolving the team's shape" or "we are right-sizing."
2. **The speaker's voice.** If the original uses "I" and short sentences, do not switch to "we" and long sentences. Match register. A founder addressing engineers should not start sounding like a presidential nominee.
3. **The structural arc.** If the original opens with bad news and ends with next steps, keep that order. CLTs amplify; they do not re-architect.
4. **Tone calibration to topic.** For sensitive topics (layoffs, illness, accidents, ethical failures), restraint is itself a charismatic signal. See "Sensitive topics" below.

## How to insert each tactic without sounding fake

For each of the 9 verbal tactics, here is how to add it gracefully into existing prose. These techniques come from the training method Antonakis et al. (2011) used.

### 1. Metaphor (Framing)
- Find an abstract noun in the draft (process, strategy, transition, growth). Replace it ONCE — not everywhere it appears — with a concrete vehicle.
- Use vehicles drawn from the speaker's world. An engineer's metaphor should come from engineering, not from sailing.
- Avoid stacking. One strong metaphor per paragraph maximum.
- A sustained metaphor across 2–3 sentences (the MLK pattern) counts as one instance and is high-impact. But it must interlock — the same conceptual frame extended — not flip vehicles mid-sentence.

### 2. Rhetorical question (Framing)
- Replace a hedging phrase ("I think the question we should be asking is whether we are…") with the bare question ("Are we building the product our customers actually want?").
- Cap at one rhetorical question per ~150 words of speech, one per email.
- Up to three in a series can work if each has a distinct sharp answer (the Anita Roddick pattern). More than three becomes a quiz.
- A rhetorical question at the *opening* of a speech sets up suspense the speech can resolve at the close — a high-leverage placement.

### 3. Story (Framing)
- Look for a place where the draft asserts a generality ("our customers are frustrated"). Add a single specific instance ("Last week, a customer named Maria emailed us at 11 p.m. saying she had been trying to reset her password for two hours") before returning to the general point.
- Keep stories ≤ 3 sentences in emails, ≤ 5 in talks. Long stories belong in keynotes only.
- A story without a payoff or distilled lesson at the end is just an anecdote. Add the moral: "That's when I realized…"

### 4. Contrast (Framing)
- Find a single declarative claim ("we are going to focus on quality"). Reframe as a contrast ("we are not going to chase every feature on the roadmap — we are going to focus on quality").
- The construction *"not X, but Y"* is the most reliable. Use it once or twice, not five times.
- Antonakis explicitly notes that contrasts are one of the easiest tactics to learn and yet are under-used. When diagnosis shows a framing gap, contrast is a strong default candidate.

### 5. List — three-part (Framing)
- Find a sentence with two coordinated items ("we will ship faster and listen more"). Add a third with parallel grammar ("we will ship faster, listen harder, and measure what matters").
- The three items should escalate or progress, not be three ways of saying the same thing.
- Lists can be announced ("There are three things…") or under the radar (just three parallel clauses). Both count.
- Cap at 2–3 three-part lists per piece. They lose force when stacked.

### 6. Moral conviction (Substance)
- Find a values-implicit sentence ("we want to do right by our team during this transition") and make the value explicit and committal ("we owe our team honesty, advance notice, and real support — and we will deliver all three").
- Pair with a constraint, a refusal, or a commitment. Empty values-speak ("we believe in excellence") is worse than nothing.
- For emails, place moral conviction at the closing — as a commitment the sender carries forward into the work.

### 7. Sentiment of the collective (Substance)
- Find the audience's most likely unspoken reaction to the content. Name it once, early ("I know many of you have been waiting for this announcement for weeks, and I know the waiting itself has been hard").
- Do not put words in their mouth that they would dispute. Don't claim they're "excited" when they're worried.
- This works "even when the sentiments are negative" (Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2012) — naming fatigue, frustration, doubt, or fear earns the right to redirect.

### 8. Ambitious goal (Substance)
- Find a soft commitment ("we hope to grow next year") and harden it into a specific, ambitious target ("by this time next year, we will be the default tool for every product team at the top 100 SaaS companies").
- **Always pair with tactic 9 (goal can be achieved).** Antonakis treats these as a functional pair. Goals without warrant read as anxiety.
- The credibility constraint (Bastardoz, 2020): "if the goal is not achievable, they lose credibility." Do not invent goals the speaker cannot defend.

### 9. Goal can be achieved (Substance)
- After every ambitious goal, add one sentence of warrant: why we will reach it. Cite team, history, momentum, or assets.
- Avoid bare cheerleading ("we can do this!"). Always anchor in something specific.
- The Machida-pairing pattern (cited in HBR, 2012): ambitious goal → personal commitment to take it on → mobilizing teams → calling it survival-critical. The warrant is concrete action, not just words.

## Category-balance principle

Aim for at least 2 framing tactics AND at least 2 substance tactics in any piece longer than ~150 words. If the draft has only framing (clever rhetoric without commitments) it reads as performance. If the draft has only substance (commitments without rhetoric) it reads as a memo. The combination is what produces the signal.

When the diagnostic shows BOTH categories thin, prioritize substance — because substance signals what the leader will *do*, and that's what an audience needs from a leadership message. When substance is adequate but framing is thin, add contrast and metaphor first (both are high-leverage and easy to insert).

## Inline annotation format

In Mode B and Mode C, return the rewrite with inline annotations using the canonical tactic names. Use this format:

```
We've spent two years building something most teams thought was impossible
[sentiment_of_the_collective][goal_can_be_achieved — implicit].
Not faster horses — a new way to move [contrast, metaphor]. Within eighteen
months, every engineering team at every Fortune 500 will have a copy on
their desktop [ambitious_goal]. We've got the product, the team, and the
timing [list][goal_can_be_achieved].
```

After the annotated rewrite, include a brief rationale (2–4 sentences) explaining what you added, what you intentionally did NOT add, and why. Reference the category balance: "I added one framing tactic (contrast) and two substance tactics (ambitious goal + goal-can-be-achieved as the canonical pair). I did not add a story or rhetorical question — the piece is short and category coverage is now adequate."

End the response with the Mode B signature line on its own line (see SKILL.md "Output signature"):

```
[clt-skill v2.1 · rewrite · added:<comma-separated canonical tactic names>]
```

## Sensitive topics: the restraint rule

For layoffs, illness, accidents, ethical failures, deaths, crises, and similarly heavy content, the calibration is opposite. The right move is FEWER, well-placed tactics, not more. This is consistent with how Antonakis et al. (2022) held moral conviction constant across their charismatic and non-charismatic conditions — the moral content of the message wasn't manipulated, only the framing was.

Specifically:
- Use **sentiment of the collective** once, early — to name what people are feeling.
- Use **moral conviction** once, to commit to a value (honesty, care, accountability).
- That's usually enough. Consider stopping there.
- Do NOT add metaphors that risk trivializing the topic ("we are turning the page on a difficult chapter" reads as evasive).
- Do NOT add rhetorical questions ("how did we get here?" sounds either flippant or accusatory).
- Do NOT add three-part lists that bundle the bad news with cheery items.
- Ambitious goals belong AFTER the acknowledgment of difficulty, not woven through it.

If the user requests aggressive CLT density on a sensitive topic, push back: "This topic warrants restraint. I can add at most two tactics here without crossing into a tone that will undermine the message."

## Worked example: applying the diagnosis

**Input draft (a manager's all-hands opening, 110 words):**

> Thanks everyone for being here. I want to talk about what we're going to focus on next quarter. We've had a lot of customer feedback recently, and a lot of it has been about reliability. So we're going to spend the next quarter mostly on reliability work. We've identified about twelve major reliability issues and we have a plan to address each of them. I know this means some feature work will be deprioritized, but I think this is the right call. We'll come back to features in Q3. Any questions?

**Diagnosis.**
- Framing balance: thin (0/5 — no metaphor, no rhetorical question, no story, no contrast, no list).
- Substance balance: 1/4 (faint moral conviction in "I think this is the right call").
- Tactics absent: metaphor, story, contrast, list, rhetorical question, sentiment of the collective, ambitious goal, goal can be achieved.
- Tactics over-represented: none.
- Highest-leverage additions for this content type (all-hands, mixed audience): sentiment of the collective (people are worried about their feature work), contrast (frame the choice clearly), ambitious goal + goal-can-be-achieved (paired — give them somewhere to aim with warrant).

**Rewrite (140 words):**

> Thanks for being here. I know some of you walked in worried that we're about to deprioritize work you've spent months on [sentiment_of_the_collective]. You're right — and I want to explain why.
>
> We are not going to spend Q2 racing the roadmap [contrast — opens]. We are going to spend Q2 making this product unbreakable [contrast — closes]. Twelve reliability issues. One quarter. A team that has shipped harder things in shorter windows [list, goal_can_be_achieved]. By the end of Q2, this product will be the most stable in its category [ambitious_goal]. That gives us a foundation to build the next year of features on top of — and that's a foundation worth pausing for [moral_conviction].
>
> Q3 we ship features. Questions?

**Rationale.** Added one tactic from framing (contrast — Antonakis's easiest to add), one from list (three-part list with implicit confidence warrant), and three from substance (sentiment of the collective, ambitious goal + goal-can-be-achieved as the canonical pair, moral conviction). Framing went from 0/5 to 2/5 (contrast + list). Substance went from 1/4 to 4/4. Did not add metaphor or rhetorical question — the topic is technical and concrete, and the piece is already balanced.

## What to do if the original is already strong

If your diagnosis finds that the original already has framing 3+/5 and substance 3+/4, well-balanced, do not force additions. Return the original largely intact with one or two surgical edits and explain in the rationale: "The draft already has strong category coverage (framing 3/5, substance 3/4). I made one small change (X) and left the rest untouched."

It is better to return a small honest improvement than to manufacture a 30% lift by overdoing it.

---

## A final reminder from the founder

Antonakis writes in HBR (2012): "No amount of training or practice will turn you into Churchill or Martin Luther King Jr. But the CLTs can make you more charismatic in the eyes of your followers, and that will invariably make you a more effective leader."

The rewriter is not trying to make the speaker Churchill. It is trying to add 2–4 well-placed tactics that close the gap between the speaker as they are and a measurably more charismatic version of the same person, on the same topic, making the same commitments.
