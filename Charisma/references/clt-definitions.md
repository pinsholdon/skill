# The 12 Charismatic Leadership Tactics — Canonical Reference

This file is the canonical reference. Every mode of the skill reads it.

The framework comes from Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011, *Academy of Management Learning & Education*), popularized in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, *Harvard Business Review*), and refined in Antonakis, Bastardoz, Jacquart & Shamir (2016, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*).

## The construct definition

> Charisma is values-based, symbolic, and emotion-laden leader signaling.
> — Antonakis, Bastardoz, Jacquart & Shamir (2016, p. 304)

The 12 tactics operationalize this signal. Per the HBR practitioner article, the framework maps onto Aristotle's three modes of persuasion: **logos** (reasoned rhetoric → framing tactics), **ethos** (moral credibility → substance tactics), and **pathos** (emotion → delivery tactics, plus the affective dimension of substance).

## The three categories

Antonakis classifies the 9 verbal tactics into two functional categories — **Framing** (how the signal is constructed) and **Substance** (what the signal commits to) — and treats the 3 nonverbal tactics as a third category — **Delivery** (how the signal is performed). This structure comes from Jacquart & Antonakis (2015) and is elaborated in Bastardoz (2020). The skill uses it everywhere because it maps to the *function* each tactic performs:

| Category | What it signals to followers | The tactics |
|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | The leader's intelligence/ability (rhetorical prowess and symbolic thinking are hard to fake) | metaphor, rhetorical question, story, contrast, list |
| **Substance** | The leader's intentions and values (what they will commit to) | moral conviction, sentiment of the collective, ambitious goal, goal can be achieved |
| **Delivery** | Alignment between the verbal signal and the body | animated voice, facial expressions, gestures |

Verbal and nonverbal CLTs correlate strongly (r ≈ .48; Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2011), so verbal-only coding captures most of the construct in text-only input.

# FRAMING (verbal tactics 1–5)

These tactics construct the signal. They demonstrate the rhetorical ability that hard-to-fake signaling theory predicts charismatic leaders will display.

## 1. Metaphor

**Definition.** A comparison that maps an abstract or technical concept onto a concrete, familiar one. Includes similes and analogies. Per Bastardoz (2020, p. 315): metaphors "transmit a message through symbols. Rather than communicating an abstract message, metaphors appeal to the imagination and emotions of the followers. By offering a vivid analog of the situation, the message becomes more salient and long-lasting." Aristotle, quoted in Antonakis et al. (2016): "the greatest thing by far is to have a command of metaphor… it is the mark of genius."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Martin Luther King Jr. likened the U.S. Constitution to a "promissory note" guaranteeing rights, then said America had given Black citizens "a bad check" marked "insufficient funds" (cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2012).
- Joe, a manager in Antonakis's training studies, introduced an urgent relocation by comparing it to "a long-awaited pregnancy" with four months instead of nine to prepare (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Frank, an IT executive in the same study, pushed back at unrealistic goals: "How can you expect me to change an engine in a plane midflight?" (combines metaphor + rhetorical question).
- Gilles, a senior VP coaching a stagnant team: "you're playing too much defense when you need to be playing more offense" (combines metaphor + contrast).

**Detection cues.**
- X-is-Y constructions where X is abstract and Y is concrete.
- "Like a…", "as if…", "imagine a…"
- A noun used outside its literal domain (war on, journey of, bridge between).
- Sustained vehicles where multiple sentences elaborate the same metaphorical frame (a hallmark of MLK's homiletic style).

**Anti-pattern.** Stacked metaphors that fight each other ("we are a phoenix rising from the ashes of our quarterly headwinds, ready to plant the seeds of tomorrow"). Worn-out business clichés ("low-hanging fruit," "move the needle") — these are dead metaphors that have lost their imagistic power. A metaphor that requires three sentences to explain is no longer doing its job.

## 2. Rhetorical question

**Definition.** A question posed not to receive an answer but to make the audience reflect, agree, or engage. Per Bastardoz (2020, p. 315): "Rhetorical questions represent an invitation to the follower to actively participate in the creation of the vision. The message becomes more valuable because the follower actively co-produces it." Rhetorical questions used at the beginning of a speech "help create a suspense that is only resolved through or at the epilog of the speech."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Martin Luther King Jr.: "There are those who are asking the devotees of civil rights, 'When will you be satisfied?'" — and then went on to answer it (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Anita Roddick, founder of The Body Shop, used three rhetorical questions to explain her social-responsibility work: "How do you make business kinder? How do you embed it in the community? How do you make community a social purpose for business?" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Mika, a manager in Antonakis's training studies, motivating an underperforming subordinate: "where do you want to go from here? Will it be back to your office feeling sorry for yourself? Or do you want to show what you are capable of achieving?"
- From the Jensen et al. (2023) coded governor speech: "What does this mean? Unemployment insurance? Will it cover the bills?"

**Detection cues.**
- A question mark in otherwise declarative content.
- Questions whose answers are implied by surrounding context.
- "Why," "what if," "how can," "imagine if" at the start of a sentence.
- Series of short questions in a row (Anita Roddick pattern, governor-speech pattern) — but see anti-pattern.

**Anti-pattern.** Stacks of rhetorical questions in a row turn the piece into a quiz. (Up to three in a clear series, with sharp answers, works — like Roddick. More than three becomes hectoring.) Questions whose answers are obvious ("Do we want to succeed?") condescend. Questions that come across as accusatory ("How did we get here?") in crisis contexts can backfire. Easy to overuse; flag if rhetorical questions exceed 25% of total CLT instances.

## 3. Story / anecdote

**Definition.** A short narrative that illustrates the point. Per Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011): stories "make the message understandable and easy to remember and induce identification with the protagonists." Per Bastardoz (2020): stories also "generate a common, agreed meaning within groups and larger collectives, creating a sense of shared identity."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Bill Gates at Harvard commencement: a story about his mother hosting a bridal event days before his wedding to Melinda, where she read a letter aloud closing with: "From those to whom much is given, much is expected" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Lynn, a manager in Antonakis's training studies, used a personal story to motivate her team during a crisis: climbing the Eiger peak with her team, getting caught in bad weather, surviving by working together. She closed: "Today we are in an economic storm, but by pulling together, we can turn this situation around and succeed" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- In Antonakis's 2011 training, two speeches were given to participants as exemplars of charismatic content: Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream" and Jesse Jackson's 1988 Democratic National Convention address.

**Detection cues.**
- A specific person, place, or moment named.
- Past-tense narrative verbs: "she told me," "I remember when," "last week, walking through…"
- A scene that can be visualized.
- An explicit "moral" or distilled lesson at the end of the narrative ("That's when I learned…").

**Anti-pattern.** A story so long it derails the speech (especially fatal in emails). A "story" that is actually a list of examples ("I've seen this with customer A, customer B, and customer C"). Stock parables ("there was once a CEO who…") used as if personal. For email: any story longer than 3 sentences. A story without a payoff — the moral or pivot point — leaves the audience asking "and?"

## 4. Contrast

**Definition.** Pairing opposing ideas to sharpen the message. Per Antonakis (2016): contrasts "define the vision in terms of what it should or should not be" and focus attention on the message. Per Bastardoz (2020): "Contrasts shape a clear opposition between two ideas and bolster the desired values by opposing two end points. Contrasts tend to create a strong effect that appeals to the emotions of listeners." Per HBR (2012): contrasts "combine reason and passion."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- John F. Kennedy's "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" (cited in HBR, 2012; described as Antonakis's most-used contrast example).
- Gilles, senior VP: "you're playing too much defense when you need to be playing more offense" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Sally, introducing herself to a new medical division team: "I asked to lead the medical division not because it has the best location but because I believe we can accomplish something great for our company and at the same time help save lives" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- From the Jensen et al. (2023) coded governor speech: "This is not a sprint, my friends, this is a marathon." "Don't be reactive, be proactive." "You don't win on defense, you win on offense."

**Detection cues.**
- The construction "not X, but Y" or "X, not Y."
- Parallel grammatical structure across two clauses with opposing content.
- Conjunctions of opposition: "but," "yet," "whereas," "rather."
- Two ideas placed in deliberate symmetry, often within a single sentence.

**Anti-pattern.** False dichotomies that insult the audience's intelligence ("we can either grow or die"). Empty word-flips that sound balanced but mean nothing ("we don't sell products, we sell experiences" — only effective if backed by substance). Contrast for rhythm with no semantic payload.

**Note from Antonakis's experience.** Antonakis writes in HBR (2012): "contrasts are one of the easiest tactics to learn and use, and yet they aren't used enough." Contrasts should be a default candidate when the diagnostic shows a framing gap.

## 5. List (especially three-part)

**Definition.** Three coordinated items — nouns, verbs, clauses — used together for rhetorical weight. Per HBR (2012): "Why three? Because most people can remember three things; three is sufficient to provide proof of a pattern, and three gives an impression of completeness." Per Bastardoz (2020): three-part lists "trigger a sense of completeness and exhaustion in the argumentation. They also facilitate recall and do not overload with information."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Serge, a midlevel manager: "We have the best product on the market. We have the best team. Yet we did not make the sales target" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Karin, division head: "First, we need to look back and see what we did right. Next, we need to see where we went wrong. Then, we need to come up with a plan that will convince the board to give us the resources to get it right the next time" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- "Of the people, by the people, for the people" — classic Lincoln pattern.
- Per HBR (2012): lists can be announced ("There are three things we need to do…") or "under the radar" — used without flagging.

**Detection cues.**
- Three items separated by commas with the last preceded by "and" or "or."
- Three parallel clauses ("we built X, we shipped Y, we measured Z").
- Three adjectives modifying one noun, especially when they escalate.
- Numbered "first… second… third…" structures.

**Anti-pattern.** Three items that are actually one idea phrased three ways ("efficient, productive, and high-performing"). Four-part lists that don't work — the rule of three breaks if you stretch it. Forced parallelism that mangles meaning. Per Antonakis: the three items should provide *proof of a pattern*, not redundancy.

# SUBSTANCE (verbal tactics 6–9)

These tactics carry the actual content of the signal — what the leader stands for and what they commit to. Per Bastardoz (2020): substance tactics signal "a leader's willingness to act on specific values and group emotions, which ultimately affects followers' efforts and investments in the task." They are costly signals over time because "leaders who do not act on their communicated intent will not be selected or followed in the future."

## 6. Moral conviction

**Definition.** A statement of values, principles, or ethical commitment. Per Antonakis (2016): moral conviction "highlights value systems and provides justification for the mission." Per Bastardoz (2020): moral conviction "refers to demonstrating faith in the moral righteousness of the message. A leader expressing moral conviction will draw followers' attention to the moral imperative of the signal, justifying why the vision should be adopted."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Winston Churchill on Victory Day at the end of the Second World War: "This is your hour. This is not victory of a party or of any class. It's a victory of the great British nation as a whole" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Tina, a manager in an NGO, pushing for supply-chain change: "It is not our donors who'll feel it, but the children we're supposed to be feeding that will go to bed one more time with an empty belly… this is not right, especially because the fix is so simple" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Gandhi statements on the inestimable value of moral support and principled action (cited in HBR, 2012).

**Detection cues.**
- "We believe," "we will not," "this is who we are."
- Naming a value as a constraint on action ("because honesty matters, we…").
- A line that, if read aloud, would warrant a slow delivery and a pause after it.
- Moral vocabulary: "right," "owe," "duty," "honor," "wrong," "fair," "responsibility."

**Anti-pattern.** Empty values-speak unconnected to action ("we believe in excellence and integrity"). Moral conviction used to disguise hard news. Three moral statements stacked in a row dilute each one. Moral language without a concrete commitment or constraint.

## 7. Sentiment of the collective

**Definition.** Naming aloud what the audience is feeling, experiencing, or worried about. Per Antonakis (2016): expressing sentiment of the collective "shows similarity of the leader with the followers." Per Bastardoz (2020): it "creates a connection between the leader's message and followers' needs… The leader is thus perceived as 'one of their own' because followers believe the leader feels the same way or at least understands their concern." Per HBR (2012): this works "even when the sentiments are negative."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Rami, a senior IT director, addressing his disheartened team: "I know what is going through your minds, because the same thing is going through mine. We all feel disappointed and demotivated. Some of you have told me you have had sleepless nights; others, that there are tensions in the team, even at home because of this. Personally, life to me has become dull and tasteless. I know how hard we have all worked and the bitterness we feel because success just slipped out of our reach. But it's not going to be like this for much longer. I have a plan" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- From Jensen et al. (2023) coded governor speech: "If you are feeling disoriented, it's not you, it's everyone, and it's everywhere, and it's with good cause." (Also includes a three-part list.)

**Detection cues.**
- "I know you," "we've all," "many of you," "you're probably thinking."
- Second-person plural addressed to a shared experience.
- Acknowledging a feeling, doubt, or fatigue the audience holds.
- "I know how you feel," "I'm with you on this," "I share your…"

**Anti-pattern.** Putting words in the audience's mouth they wouldn't endorse ("I know you all share my excitement about the new reporting structure" — when they don't). Forced "I'm one of you" claims from someone clearly not one of them. Performative empathy not followed by responsive action.

## 8. Ambitious goal

**Definition.** An ambitious destination — bigger than incremental. Per Antonakis (2016): setting ambitious goals "shows ambition, aligns efforts towards goals." Per Bastardoz (2020): "Setting high and ambitious goals shows a leader's ambition, commitment to the vision, and willingness to achieve the goals. This signal motivates followers to engage in costly but valuable efforts, increasing their self-esteem and competence."

Crucial caveat from Bastardoz (2020): "A leader's vision needs to be appropriate and realizable because if the goal is not achievable, they lose credibility." Ambitious goals are a costly signal because they put the leader's credibility on the line.

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Gandhi's almost-impossible goal of liberating India from British rule without violence — the "quit India" speech (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Katsuhiko Machida, former CEO of Sharp, in 1998 when Sharp faced collapse and LCD technology was commercially unviable: "By 2005, all TVs we sell in Japan will be LCD models" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- John F. Kennedy's man-on-the-moon ambition framed against a specific decade.

**Detection cues.**
- A target — number, milestone, transformation — that strains plausibility.
- A timeframe attached to a non-trivial outcome.
- Verbs of ambition: "transform," "redefine," "eliminate," "become."

**Anti-pattern.** Goals so high they read as fiction ("we will end loneliness by Q3"). Goals with no timeframe ("we will eventually…"). Goals stated as desires rather than commitments ("we hope to maybe…"). Setting an ambitious goal without tactic #9 (goal-can-be-achieved) leaves the audience anxious instead of inspired — these two work as a pair.

---

## 9. Goal can be achieved

**Definition.** Conveying confidence that the ambitious goal is reachable. Per Antonakis (2016): "creating confidence goals can be achieved" raises followers' self-efficacy belief. Per Bastardoz (2020): "Doing so increases followers' confidence in the achievability of the leader's signal. Because followers believe that their investment is worthy and can succeed, they are more likely to be motivated and exhibit costly efforts."

**Examples drawn from Antonakis's own published worked cases.**
- Gandhi: "I know the British Government will not be able to withhold freedom from us, when we have made enough self-sacrifice" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Gandhi, more forcefully later: "Even if all the United Nations opposes me, even if the whole of India forsakes me, I will say, 'You are wrong. India will wrench with nonviolence her liberty from unwilling hands'" (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Machida (paired with his ambitious LCD goal): personally went to Sharp's engineers, made it the company's most important project, brought together cross-functional teams from LCD and TV development (cited in HBR, 2012).
- Ray, an engineer in Antonakis's training studies, addressing his team after a setback: "The deadline the CEO gave us is daunting. Other teams would be right to tremble at the knees, but we are not just another team. I know you can rise to the challenge" (cited in HBR, 2012).

**Detection cues.**
- Affirmations of capacity ("we have what it takes").
- Past evidence cited as warrant for future success.
- "Will" (not "might," "could," "hope to") attached to the goal.
- Calm certainty in the syntax — short, declarative sentences.

**Anti-pattern.** Bare cheerleading without warrant ("we can do this!"). Confidence statements that promise certainty no one can deliver ("there is zero risk of failure"). Confidence so loud it sounds defensive. Always pair with at least a hint of evidence — "we have the team" rather than just "we will succeed."

# DELIVERY (nonverbal tactics 10–12, audio/video only)

When scoring plain text, skip these three and state in the output: *"Nonverbal tactics not scored — input is text only."*

Per Bastardoz (2020): delivery tactics "entail nonverbal behaviors that align with the leader's verbal signals." The key principle is **alignment**: the body should reinforce, not contradict, what the words are doing.

## 10. Animated voice

**Definition.** Variation in pitch, volume, tempo, and emphasis. Per HBR (2012): "People who are passionate vary the volume with which they speak — whispering at appropriate points or rising to a crescendo to hammer home a point. Emotion — sadness, happiness, excitement, surprise — must come through in the voice. Pauses are also important because they convey control."

**Detection cues (video/audio).** Range in pitch across a sentence. Strategic pauses before key words. Volume that rises with importance and falls into intimacy. Tempo that slows on the most important phrase.

**Anti-pattern.** Monotone delivery (under-use). Singsong cadence that turns every sentence into the same melody (over-use). Volume changes uncorrelated with content. Pauses as filler rather than emphasis.

---

## 11. Facial expressions

**Definition.** Expression that matches the emotional content of the message. Per HBR (2012): "These help reinforce your message. Listeners need to see as well as hear your passion — especially when you're telling a story or reflecting their sentiments. So be sure to make eye contact (one of the givens of charisma), and get comfortable smiling, frowning, and laughing at work."

**Detection cues (video).** Eyebrow movement coordinated with key phrases. Smile lines for warmth, jaw set for resolve. Eye contact held through emotionally important sentences. Expression that shifts with content.

**Anti-pattern.** A frozen face during emotionally charged content. Smiling while delivering bad news. Theatrical micro-expressions that look rehearsed. A serious expression held so rigidly it reads as anger.

---

## 12. Gestures

**Definition.** Purposeful hand and arm movement. Per HBR (2012): "These are signals for your listeners. A fist can reinforce confidence, power, and certitude. Waving a hand, pointing, or pounding a desk can help draw attention."

**Detection cues (video).** Open hands during inclusive language. Pointing for direction or emphasis. Hand-counting for three-part lists. Gestures that arrive ON the keyword, not before or after.

**Anti-pattern.** Hands locked at sides (under-use). Wild flailing (over-use). Repetitive nervous gestures (pen-clicking, hair-touching). Aggressive pointing at the audience.

# Quick reference: tactic priority by content type

When deciding what to add in Mode B / Mode C, prioritize:

| Content type | First-choice tactics |
|---|---|
| Keynote / inspirational speech | story, metaphor, three-part list, ambitious goal + goal-can-be-achieved (always paired) |
| All-hands / town hall | sentiment of the collective, moral conviction, ambitious goal + goal-can-be-achieved |
| Crisis / bad news | sentiment of the collective, moral conviction, goal-can-be-achieved (sparingly) |
| Sales pitch | contrast, rhetorical question, metaphor, goal-can-be-achieved |
| Email — request | rhetorical question, three-part list, moral conviction |
| Email — bad news | sentiment of the collective (one line), moral conviction (one line). Stop. |
| Email — call to action | contrast, three-part list, goal-can-be-achieved |
| LinkedIn post | metaphor, contrast, story (≤3 sentences), rhetorical question |

# A note from the founder's own training

Antonakis writes in HBR (2012) that participants in his training initially worried they sounded inauthentic — what psychologists call the *"illusion of transparency."* After the second filming session, they felt comfortable, and crucially: independent raters debriefing on the videos did not suspect the participants were "putting on an act." The lesson for the skill: when a user receives a rewrite that feels exaggerated to them, that may be the illusion of transparency at work — but if it triggers the human-voice guardrail, trust the guardrail, not the feeling.
