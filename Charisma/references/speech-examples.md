# Speech Examples — Drawn from Antonakis's Published Worked Cases

This file teaches pattern-matching by example. Unlike v1, the examples here come from cases Antonakis himself uses in his published work — primarily the HBR practitioner article (Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2012), the AMLE training paper (Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti, 2011), the coded speech vignette from Jensen et al. (2023), and the Antonakis et al. (2022) "Just Words?" experiment.

For each example, short quotes (under 15 words) are verbatim from the cited source; longer passages are paraphrased reconstructions in the style of the speaker, with the verbatim phrasing preserved.

---

## EXAMPLE 1 — The MLK metaphor pattern (sustained, interlocking vehicles)

**Source.** Cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR) as Antonakis's most-used metaphor exemplar.

**Pattern reconstruction.** Martin Luther King Jr. likened the U.S. Constitution to a promissory note guaranteeing the unalienable rights of life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness to all people. He then said America had given its Black citizens "a bad check," one that came back marked "insufficient funds" [metaphor — sustained vehicle]. Everyone knows what it means to receive a bad check [metaphor — concretizes the abstract].

**What's at work.** This is the *sustained metaphor* pattern. A single conceptual frame (financial instrument) is extended across multiple sentences. Each subsequent mention deepens rather than abandons the original vehicle. Antonakis specifically praises this technique: "Everyone knows what it means to receive a bad check. The message is crystal clear and easy to retain" (HBR 2012). The mark of a strong metaphor in the MLK register is that it survives extension — you can keep building on it.

**When to use.** Speeches with high moral or symbolic stakes. Closing sections of keynotes. Sermon-like structures. Avoid in technical or transactional contexts.

## EXAMPLE 2 — The JFK contrast pattern (parallel antithesis)

**Source.** Cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR) as the canonical contrast example.

**Verbatim.** "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" [contrast — parallel antithesis].

**What's at work.** Symmetric grammatical structure on either side of the dash. The opposition isn't false — it's a genuine reorientation of orientation (from receiving to giving). The rhythm carries the meaning. The construction *"ask not X — ask Y"* can be ported to any context where the speaker wants to reframe a default assumption.

**When to use.** Strategic reframing. Opening lines. Closing lines. Any moment where a familiar question needs to be replaced with a better one. Per Antonakis: contrasts are "one of the easiest tactics to learn and use, and yet they aren't used enough."

## EXAMPLE 3 — Bill Gates story pattern (personal narrative with a payoff)

**Source.** Bill Gates's Harvard commencement, cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR) as the canonical short-story example.

**Pattern.** Bill Gates told of his mother who never stopped pressing him to do more for others [story — opens]. A few days before his wedding, she hosted a bridal event where she read aloud a letter she had written to Melinda about marriage [story — concrete scene]. His mother was ill with cancer at the time but found one more opportunity to deliver her message [story — emotional stake]. She closed the letter: "From those to whom much is given, much is expected" [moral_conviction — distilled lesson].

**What's at work.** This is the *short-story-with-payoff* structure. Three or four sentences set a specific scene; the closing line distills the moral. The closing maxim is what makes the story do its work — without it, the story is just an anecdote. Note also that the story carries moral conviction as its payoff, so a single short narrative can contribute to both framing and substance categories.

**When to use.** Opening of inspirational talks. Pivot moments in talks where you need the audience to feel the weight of what comes next. NEVER in emails unless the story is compressed to a single sentence.

## EXAMPLE 4 — The Anita Roddick rhetorical-question pattern (three-question series)

**Source.** Anita Roddick on social responsibility, cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR).

**Verbatim.** "How do you make business kinder? How do you embed it in the community? How do you make community a social purpose for business?" [rhetorical_question × 3 — series]

**What's at work.** Three rhetorical questions in a row, each escalating in scope. The first is the immediate question; the second extends; the third synthesizes. This is the maximum dose — more than three in series becomes a quiz. Each question must have a distinct, sharp implicit answer for the series to work. When the questions are vague or rhetorically similar, the series collapses into hectoring.

**When to use.** Opening of a talk where you want to introduce a problem space. Setup for a closing answer that resolves all three questions at once. Avoid in emails — three questions in an email reads as combative.

## EXAMPLE 5 — The Serge three-part list (under-the-radar)

**Source.** Serge, a midlevel manager in Antonakis's training studies, cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR).

**Verbatim.** "We have the best product on the market. We have the best team. Yet we did not make the sales target" [list — three-part, under-the-radar, with a turn].

**What's at work.** Three parallel clauses — same opening ("We have…" / "We have…" / "Yet we…"). The third item subverts the pattern: instead of a third positive claim, it pivots to the problem. This is a sophisticated use of the three-part list pattern — the rhythm sets up the expectation, the third element delivers the news. The list is *under the radar* — there's no announcement ("there are three things…") but the syntactic parallelism is unmistakable.

**When to use.** When you want to deliver hard news after acknowledgments. The "Yes, Yes, But" pattern in compressed form. Excellent in town-hall openings that need to move from team appreciation to the hard topic.

## EXAMPLE 6 — The Rami sentiment-of-the-collective pattern (named feelings, named details)

**Source.** Rami, a senior IT director in Antonakis's training studies, cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR).

**Verbatim.** "I know what is going through your minds, because the same thing is going through mine. We all feel disappointed and demotivated. Some of you have told me you have had sleepless nights; others, that there are tensions in the team, even at home because of this. Personally, life to me has become dull and tasteless. I know how hard we have all worked and the bitterness we feel because success just slipped out of our reach. But it's not going to be like this for much longer. I have a plan" [sentiment_of_the_collective — sustained acknowledgment, multiple specific details].

**What's at work.** Notice the *specific details*. Not "I know you're frustrated" — but "sleepless nights," "tensions in the team, even at home," "life to me has become dull and tasteless." The leader has done the work of asking and listening. The closing line ("I have a plan") is the pivot — sentiment of the collective earns the right to redirect, and you must redirect or the message wallows.

**When to use.** All-hands openings during difficult periods. Internal messages after a setback, missed target, or extended hard stretch. The structure: name what they're feeling with specificity → confess you're feeling it too → signal the redirect.

## EXAMPLE 7 — The Gandhi ambitious-goal + goal-can-be-achieved pair

**Source.** Gandhi's "Quit India" speech, cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR) as the canonical ambitious-goal example.

**Pattern.** Gandhi set the almost impossible (and moral) goal of liberating India from British rule without using violence [ambitious_goal]. He then said: "I know the British Government will not be able to withhold freedom from us, when we have made enough self-sacrifice" [goal_can_be_achieved — with warrant: self-sacrifice]. In a later speech he doubled down: "Even if all the United Nations opposes me, even if the whole of India forsakes me, I will say, 'You are wrong'" [goal_can_be_achieved — conviction-grounded warrant].

**What's at work.** The pair. The ambitious goal (liberation by nonviolent means) is wild on its own. The warrant ("when we have made enough self-sacrifice") is what makes it credible. Without the warrant, the goal sounds like dreaming. With it, the goal becomes a commitment with a path. Note that Gandhi's warrant is not bare cheerleading ("we can do it!") — it points to a specific mechanism: self-sacrifice.

**When to use.** Anywhere you state an ambitious goal. The two tactics travel as a pair. The rewriter should rarely add tactic 8 without also adding tactic 9.

## EXAMPLE 8 — The Machida corporate parallel

**Source.** Katsuhiko Machida, then CEO of Sharp, in 1998. Cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR).

**Pattern.** In 1998, Sharp faced collapse, cathode-ray tubes dominated the TV market, and LCD technology was commercially unviable. Machida energized his employees by stating the unthinkable: "By 2005, all TVs we sell in Japan will be LCD models" [ambitious_goal — specific, dated, defies market reality]. He then personally took his vision to Sharp's engineers to convince them they could realize his risky goal, made it the company's most important project, brought together cross-functional teams from LCD and TV development, and told them plainly that it was crucial to Sharp's survival [goal_can_be_achieved — by concrete personal commitment, organizational mobilization, and stakes].

**What's at work.** This is the corporate equivalent of the Gandhi pair. The goal is specific (date, market, product line). The warrant is *action*, not words — Machida didn't just declare; he reorganized the company around the goal. For a charismatic-leadership skill, the lesson is that goal-can-be-achieved is most credible when it points to specific commitments the leader is making, not abstract assertions of belief.

**When to use.** When the user is delivering an ambitious commercial or organizational goal. The Machida pattern is more portable to corporate contexts than the Gandhi pattern.

## EXAMPLE 9 — The Churchill moral-conviction pattern

**Source.** Winston Churchill on Victory Day, end of the Second World War. Cited in Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2012, HBR).

**Verbatim segments.** "This is your hour. This is not victory of a party or of any class. It's a victory of the great British nation as a whole" [moral_conviction — naming the value of collective ownership]. "There we stood, alone. The lights went out and the bombs came down" [story — compressed]. "But every man, woman, and child in the country had no thought of quitting the struggle" [moral_conviction, list — three-part, sentiment_of_the_collective].

**What's at work.** Multiple tactics in a small space, but each clearly distinguishable, and the moral conviction (collective ownership of victory) is the load-bearing element. Note the *give-it-away* move: Churchill explicitly disclaims ownership ("not victory of a party or of any class"). Moral conviction in a leader's mouth carries more weight when it constrains the leader, not when it inflates them.

**When to use.** Recognition speeches. Year-end addresses. Moments after a sustained collective effort. The "this belongs to all of us" pattern.

## EXAMPLE 10 — The Antonakis-coded governor speech vignette (Jensen et al. 2023)

**Source.** A real coded speech vignette from Jensen et al. (2023, *Leadership Quarterly*), showing 18 sentences with sentence-level CLT codings. This is an authentic, published example.

**The vignette, with the published codings shown inline.**

> 1. "This is not a sprint, my friends, this is a marathon." [contrast, metaphor]
> 2. "You have to gauge yourself." [no tactic]
> 3. "And even though it's so disruptive, and so abrupt, and so shocking, it's also long-term." [list — three-part]
> 4. "Stay six feet away from people." [no tactic]
> 5. "Don't be reactive, be proactive." [contrast]
> 6. "There's economic anxiety, people are out of work." [no tactic]
> 7. "What does this mean?" [rhetorical_question — opens a series that runs through s.8 and s.9]
> 8. "Unemployment insurance?" [rhetorical_question]
> 9. "Will it cover the bills?" [rhetorical_question]
> 10. "This fear of the unknown, this misinformation." [no tactic]
> 11. "You put it all together, it is very disorienting, to say the least." [no tactic]
> 12. "If you are feeling disoriented, it's not you, it's everyone, and it's everywhere, and it's with good cause." [sentiment_of_the_collective, list — three-part]
> 13. "Also, we have to plan forward on testing." [no tactic]
> 14. "We've mobilized, we've scrambled, but this is still not where it needs to be." [no tactic]
> 15. "We need many more tests." [no tactic]
> 16. "The social distancing is important." [no tactic]
> 17. "You don't win on defense, you win on offense." [contrast, metaphor]
> 18. "Don't get complacent." [no tactic]

**Coded totals from Jensen et al. (2023).** metaphor: 2 / rhetorical_question: 3 / story: 0 / contrast: 4 / list: 2 / moral_conviction: 0 / sentiment_of_the_collective: 1 / ambitious_goal: 0 / goal_can_be_achieved: 0. Total instances: 12.

**Scoring of this vignette using the skill's rubric.**
- 18 sentences, ~120 words.
- Sentences with at least one CLT: 7 of 18 = 39%.
- Variety: 5 of 9 distinct tactics present (metaphor, rhetorical question, contrast, list, sentiment of the collective). variety_ratio = 0.56.
- Framing: 4/5 present (only story missing). Substance: 1/4 present (only sentiment of the collective).
- Dominant tactic: contrast (4 / 12 = 33% — just over the 30% over-representation threshold).
- max_share = 0.33; balance_factor = 0.96.
- proportion_ratio = 0.39 / 0.90 = 0.43.
- overall_score = round(60 × 0.56 + 30 × 0.43 + 10 × 0.96) = round(33.6 + 12.9 + 9.6) = **56 — Solid**.

**What's at work.** This is a real example of moderately charismatic governmental communication. Strong framing (heavy on contrast and rhetorical questions); weak substance (no moral conviction, no ambitious goal/can-be-achieved pair). The diagnostic for a rewrite would be: substance is the gap. Add a moral conviction line ("we owe each other patience and care") and a paired goal sentence ("we will have widespread testing by the end of the month — and we will, because we have the labs, the supply chain, and the will"). Result would push proportion past 50% and substance from 1/4 to 3/4.

## EXAMPLE 11 — A leadership email (request) — constructed in the playbook style

> Subject: A small ask, with a real reason
>
> I know everyone's calendar is full this week [sentiment_of_the_collective]. I'm going to ask for thirty minutes anyway, because what we decide on Thursday will shape the next two quarters [moral_conviction].
>
> Three options are on the table: hold, accelerate, or pivot [list]. Each one closes off the other two [contrast]. If we choose right, this becomes the project we point to next year [ambitious_goal, goal_can_be_achieved].
>
> Thursday, 2 p.m. — your input matters.

**What's at work.** 5 tactics in ~85 words. Framing 2/5 (list, contrast); Substance 4/4 (moral conviction, sentiment of the collective, ambitious goal, goal can be achieved). Note that the ambitious goal and goal-can-be-achieved are bundled into a single sentence — fine for email length. The opening sentiment of the collective is the email's most important sentence; without it, the email reads as imposition rather than ask.

## EXAMPLE 12 — A leadership email (bad news) — restraint applied

> Subject: An update on the platform team
>
> I want to tell you directly, before you hear it elsewhere [moral_conviction]. We are reducing the platform team by four people, effective next Friday.
>
> I know this will land hard, especially for those of you who have been on this team since the early days [sentiment_of_the_collective]. You deserve specifics, fairness, and a real path forward. You will have all three from me, in person, this week [list, moral_conviction — promise structure].
>
> I will be in the office tomorrow. My door is open.

**What's at work.** This is the *restraint* model. Only three distinct tactics — moral conviction (twice, but bookending naturally rather than stacking), sentiment of the collective, and a three-part list that functions as a commitment. No metaphor (would trivialize). No contrast (would feel manipulative). No ambitious goal (the audience is not ready). The moral conviction at the top earns the right to deliver the news. The three-part list at the close commits the speaker to three concrete obligations — it's a *promise structure*, not a rhetorical flourish.

## How to use these examples while rewriting

When you are about to insert a tactic into a draft, find the closest example above by content type:

- Heavy moral content → Example 1 (MLK sustained metaphor) and Example 9 (Churchill)
- Strategic reframing → Example 2 (JFK contrast)
- Personal / introspective opening → Example 3 (Bill Gates story)
- Setting up a problem space → Example 4 (Anita Roddick questions)
- Mixed news in town hall opening → Example 5 (Serge under-the-radar list)
- After a setback or hard stretch → Example 6 (Rami sentiment) and Example 10 (governor)
- Ambitious commitment with credibility → Example 7 (Gandhi) and Example 8 (Machida)
- Recognition / collective ownership → Example 9 (Churchill)
- Email request → Example 11
- Email bad news → Example 12

Use the example as a calibration anchor. If your rewrite has more tactic instances per sentence than the closest example, reconsider.
